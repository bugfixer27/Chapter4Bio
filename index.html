Powers of ten

The size range of cells

From a person to an atom.

Each step down the ruler on the right is ten times smaller than the last. Ten steps take you from your own height to the width of a carbon atom.

1 mm = 10⁻³ m. 1 µm (micrometre) = 10⁻⁶ m. 1 nm (nanometre) = 10⁻⁹ m. The scale is logarithmic so that all of it fits.

1 m → 1 mm

Some cells are enormous.

A single nerve cell can run from your spine to your toes, about a metre. A chicken egg and a frog egg are single cells too, swollen with yolk to feed an embryo.

The unaided eye resolves about 0.1 mm. Everything below this point needs a microscope.

10–100 µm

The band where cells live.

A human egg, about 100 µm, is at the very edge of sight. Most plant and animal cells are 10–100 µm across, and the nucleus inside them averages about 5 µm.

Answer · B · about 1 µm

Bacteria and mitochondria: the same size.

Most bacteria are 1–5 µm; a mitochondrion is 1–10 µm long. The smallest cells known, the bacteria called mycoplasmas, are 0.1–1 µm: about the least that can hold enough DNA and enzymes to live.

Hold that resemblance. It is not a coincidence (§4.5).

Below the light microscope

Viruses, ribosomes, proteins.

Viruses (tens to hundreds of nm) and ribosomes (about 25 nm) are too small to resolve with light. A protein is a few nanometres; a lipid, smaller still.

≈ 0.1 nm

Small molecules, then atoms.

A sugar such as glucose is under a nanometre. An atom is about a tenth of a nanometre: a hundred-thousandth the width of a cell.

Its nucleus is 100 000 times smaller again, far too small to draw at this scale.

Size, on a log scale

Takeaway

  1. Most cells are 1–100 µm: too small to see, too large to be molecules.
  2. Bacteria 1–5 µm; eukaryotic cells 10–100 µm; mitochondria about bacterial size.
  3. Eye ≈ 0.1 mm; light microscope ≈ 200 nm; electron microscope ≈ 2 nm.
4.1 · Fig. 4.3

Seeing cells

Microscopy

Three numbers decide what you can see.

Magnification: image size over real size; a light microscope manages about 1 000×. Resolution: the smallest distance at which two points still look like two. Contrast: how different neighbouring parts look.

Microscopes were invented in 1590. Antoni van Leeuwenhoek's lenses first showed living cells; in 1674 he showed Hooke his “very little animalcules”.

Resolution · right panel

Two points, closing in.

Light blurs every point into a small disc. Closer than about 0.2 µm (200 nm), two discs merge into one, however much you magnify. Shorter waves resolve finer detail.

Resolution is inversely related to the wavelength used. Electrons have far shorter wavelengths than visible light.

Answer · B · Light microscopy (LM)

The same cell, through four light microscopes.

Brightfield: unstained, nearly invisible; stained, full of contrast, but fixed and dead. Phase-contrast and DIC amplify differences in density, so living cells show up unstained.

More magnification can't beat the resolution limit: the ribosomes stay a blur. DIC (Nomarski) makes the image look almost 3-D.

Fluorescence · confocal

Light up one kind of molecule.

Fluorescent dyes or antibodies label specific molecules, which absorb ultraviolet and emit visible light. Confocal microscopy uses a laser to throw away out-of-focus light: one sharp optical section (right).

Stacks of sections rebuild a cell in 3-D. Super-resolution microscopy now distinguishes structures only 10–20 nm across.

Electron microscopy (EM) · 1950s

Electrons instead of light.

An electron microscope focuses a beam of electrons with electromagnets. In practice it resolves about 2 nm: 100× better than light. The scanning EM (SEM) shows a surface in 3-D; here, the cell's microvilli.

SEM samples are coated with a thin film of gold. The beam knocks secondary electrons out of the surface; edges and slopes send out the most, so they look brightest.

Transmission EM (TEM)

A thin slice, stained with heavy metal.

The TEM fires electrons through a very thin section. Heavy-metal atoms stick to some structures and scatter electrons, so membranes, chromatin and ribosomes print dark.

EM preparation kills cells and can create artifacts, structures that do not exist in the living cell. Cryo-EM freezes specimens instead; it has resolved single proteins.

Cytology + biochemistry

Seeing is only half of it.

Microscopes are the tools of cytology, the study of cell structure. Knowing what each structure does needed biochemistry too, the study of the cell's chemical processes.

4.1 · Cell fractionation

Taking cells apart

Break, then spin

Sorting a cell's parts by size.

Cells are broken up and the soup, a homogenate, goes into a tube spun in a centrifuge. The force sends the largest components to the bottom first, into a pellet.

Each spin's liquid, the supernatant, is poured into a fresh tube and spun faster and longer.

1 000 g · 10 min

First out: nuclei.

At low speed only the biggest pieces settle: the nuclei. The tube swings out on the rotor, so “down” is outward.

g is the force as a multiple of Earth's gravity. The speeds here are typical values.

Function from fractions

How mitochondria got their job.

Biochemical tests found the enzymes of cellular respiration in one fraction. The electron microscope showed that fraction was full of mitochondria. Together: mitochondria are the sites of respiration.

Two kinds of cell

Common to all cells

Four things every cell has.

A plasma membrane, a selective barrier. Cytosol, a semifluid, jellylike interior. Chromosomes carrying genes as DNA. Ribosomes that make proteins.

Prokaryotic · Bacteria and Archaea

No nucleus: a nucleoid.

A prokaryote's DNA lies in a nucleoid, a region with no membrane around it. Outside the membrane: a rigid cell wall, often a sticky capsule, fimbriae to attach and flagella to swim.

Pro-karyotic: “before nucleus”. Eu-karyotic: “true nucleus”. The capsule or slime layer is the glycocalyx.

Eukaryotic · protists, fungi, animals, plants

Ten times wider; a thousand times the volume.

Pull back: the bacterium sits beside a eukaryotic cell. Most of its DNA is in a nucleus with a double membrane, and its cytoplasm holds membrane-bound organelles.

Bacteria are typically 1–5 µm; eukaryotic cells 10–100 µm. Cytoplasm means everything between the nucleus and the plasma membrane.

Answer · B · Fig. 4.6

Surface grows as the square, volume as the cube.

One 5-unit cube has a surface of 150 and a volume of 125. Cut into 125 unit cubes, the volume is still 125, but the surface is 750. The ratio goes from 1.2 to 6.

Every amber face is new surface the cut exposed.

Why cells are small

The membrane is the bottleneck.

Each square micrometre of membrane can pass only so much oxygen, food and waste per second. Big organisms have more cells, not bigger ones. Long thin cells such as neurons keep the ratio high, and microvilli add surface with almost no volume.

Compartments

Membranes make rooms.

Internal membranes divide a eukaryotic cell into compartments, so incompatible chemistry can run at once. Many enzymes are built into the membranes themselves.

Each membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with its own mix of proteins, the subject of chapter 5.

    The nucleus

    Information central

    A double membrane, full of holes.

    The nuclear envelope is two membranes, each a lipid bilayer, 20–40 nm apart. It is perforated by nuclear pores about 100 nm wide. At the lip of each pore the inner and outer membranes join.

    The nucleus holds most of the cell's genes; a few are in mitochondria and chloroplasts.

    Pore complexes

    A gate with a guard.

    A pore complex, an eight-fold ring of proteins, lines every pore. It lets proteins, RNAs and even ribosomal subunits through, and regulates what enters and leaves.

    Through the pore

    Inside: a net, and the DNA.

    The nuclear lamina, a net of protein filaments (intermediate filaments, in animal cells), lines the inside of the envelope and holds the nucleus in shape. Beyond it lies the DNA, as chromatin.

    Chromatin

    DNA wound on protein spools.

    Each chromosome is one long DNA molecule with many proteins, among them small basic proteins called histones, which coil the DNA so it fits. This DNA–protein complex is chromatin.

    Here, DNA (2 nm wide) wraps about 1.7 turns round each histone core: a nucleosome, about 10 nm across.

    Answer · B · Condensation

    Coil, loop, condense.

    In a dividing cell the chromatin coils further, until each chromosome is thick enough to see in a light microscope. A typical human cell has 46 chromosomes; eggs and sperm have 23.

    In a non-dividing cell, stained chromatin looks like a diffuse mass, though the chromosomes are all still there.

    The nucleolus

    A ribosome factory with no membrane.

    In the nucleolus, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is made from DNA and joined with proteins imported from the cytoplasm into large and small subunits. They leave through the pores, as does mRNA, carrying a gene's message.

    Ribosomes

    Protein factories

    Two subunits and a message.

    A ribosome is rRNA and protein, in a large subunit and a small subunit. In the cytoplasm the two clamp onto an mRNA and translate it into a polypeptide.

    Ribosomes have no membrane, so they are not counted as organelles.

    Free ribosomes

    Proteins for the cytosol.

    Free ribosomes float in the cytosol. Most of what they make works there, such as the enzymes that begin breaking down sugar.

    Answer · B · Bound ribosomes

    Threaded into the ER.

    Bound ribosomes sit on the outside of the ER or the nuclear envelope. The growing chain is threaded through a pore made of protein in the ER membrane into the lumen, where it folds.

    Bound and free ribosomes are identical, and can swap roles. A pancreas cell, which secretes digestive enzymes, has a few million ribosomes.

    Glycoproteins

    Sugar, added inside.

    Most secretory proteins are glycoproteins. Enzymes built into the ER membrane attach carbohydrates to them inside the lumen, and the ER membrane keeps them apart from the proteins of the cytosol.

    Membrane proteins

    Some stay in the membrane.

    Proteins destined to be membrane proteins are inserted into the ER membrane itself as they grow, anchored by their hydrophobic parts.

    Endoplasmic reticulum

    The endomembrane system

    One membrane system, many rooms.

    Nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles and the plasma membrane: joined directly, or by vesicles, sacs of membrane that bud from one and fuse with another.

    The membranes are related but not identical; each is modified on the way.

    The ER

    More than half the cell's membrane.

    The endoplasmic reticulum (“little net within the cytoplasm”) is a network of tubules and sacs, cisternae, around one continuous space, the lumen. The lumen runs on into the space inside the nuclear envelope.

    Rough ER

    Studded with ribosomes.

    Rough ER makes secretory proteins and is the cell's membrane factory: it adds proteins and phospholipids to its own membrane and ships the surplus off as transport vesicles from transitional ER.

    Smooth ER

    No ribosomes, other jobs.

    Smooth ER makes lipids (oils, new phospholipids, steroids such as sex hormones), metabolizes carbohydrates, detoxifies drugs and poisons, and stores calcium ions.

    Cells that make steroid hormones, in the testes and ovaries, are rich in smooth ER.

    Ca²⁺ store · muscle

    A spring loaded with calcium.

    In muscle cells the smooth ER pumps calcium ions from the cytosol into its lumen. When a nerve impulse arrives, Ca²⁺ rushes back out into the cytosol and triggers contraction.

    The Golgi apparatus

    Shipping and receiving

    A stack of flattened sacs.

    The Golgi apparatus is a stack of cisternae, like pita bread, not connected to each other. Its cis face, near the ER, receives vesicles; its trans face ships them out.

    A cell can have hundreds of stacks. Secreting cells have the most.

    Finishing work

    Modified on the way through.

    From cis to trans, teams of enzymes in each cisterna modify the cargo: sugars on glycoproteins are removed and replaced; phospholipids are altered. The Golgi makes polysaccharides too, such as the pectins of plant cell walls.

    Cisternal maturation

    The sacs themselves move.

    Vesicles from the ER fuse into a new cis cisterna. Each cisterna matures and moves toward the trans face carrying its cargo, while vesicles carry enzymes backward to younger cisternae and some proteins back to the ER.

    The old view had fixed cisternae and vesicles shuttling cargo between them. The truth may lie between: stable centres, dynamic rims.

    Lysosomes and vacuoles

    Digestive compartments

    A sac of acid and enzymes.

    A lysosome is a membrane sac of hydrolytic enzymes that work best in its acidic interior. If one leaks, its enzymes are nearly inactive at the cytosol's neutral pH; only mass leakage would digest the cell.

    Its enzymes and membrane are made by rough ER and processed in the Golgi. Their folded shapes protect them from each other.

    Phagocytosis · Fig. 4.12

    Engulf, then digest.

    A protist or a macrophage engulfs prey into a food vacuole. A lysosome fuses with it, and its enzymes break the food into sugars, amino acids and other monomers that pass into the cytosol.

    Autophagy · Fig. 4.13

    Recycling itself.

    A damaged organelle is wrapped in a double membrane; a lysosome fuses with it and dismantles it, returning the pieces to the cytosol. A human liver cell recycles half its macromolecules every week.

    Answer · A · Storage diseases

    Engorged lysosomes.

    In inherited lysosomal storage diseases a hydrolytic enzyme is missing, and lysosomes swell with what they can't digest. In Tay-Sachs disease lipids build up in the brain's cells.

    Vacuoles

    Large vesicles, many jobs.

    Vacuoles come from the ER and Golgi. Food vacuoles form by phagocytosis; contractile vacuoles pump excess water out of freshwater protists. A plant's central vacuole holds cell sap, stores ions, may hold poisons or pigments, and grows the cell by taking up water.

    Endosymbiosis

    Energy converters

    Two organelles that change energy's form.

    Mitochondria carry out cellular respiration: with oxygen, they turn the energy in sugars and fats into ATP. Chloroplasts, in plants and algae, carry out photosynthesis: sunlight into the chemical energy of sugar.

    Evolution · the endosymbiont theory

    Swallowed, but not digested.

    An early ancestor of eukaryotic cells engulfed an oxygen-using prokaryote. Instead of being digested, it lived on inside: an endosymbiont. Over many generations, host and guest merged into one organism, and the guest became the mitochondrion.

    A second merger

    Then, in one lineage, a photosynthesizer.

    At least one of these cells later took in a photosynthetic prokaryote, the ancestor of every chloroplast.

    The text discusses this in detail in Concept 25.1.

    Power plants

    Mitochondrion · Fig. 4.17

    A smooth membrane round a folded one.

    The outer membrane is smooth. The inner membrane folds into cristae and divides the inside in two: the narrow intermembrane space and the matrix.

    1–10 µm long. Some cells have hundreds or thousands; cells that move or contract have the most.

    Structure fits function

    Folds are surface.

    The matrix holds enzymes, mitochondrial DNA and free ribosomes. Other proteins of respiration, including the enzyme that makes ATP, are built into the inner membrane: the cristae give them room.

    Living mitochondria

    A network in motion.

    Time-lapse films show mitochondria moving, changing shape, fusing and dividing: a branched tubular network, not the static beans of a micrograph.

    Chloroplast · Fig. 4.18

    Stacks of green poker chips.

    Inside a double envelope, flattened sacs called thylakoids are stacked into grana. The fluid around them, the stroma, holds the chloroplast's DNA, ribosomes and enzymes.

    Lens-shaped, 3–6 µm. Three compartments: intermembrane space, stroma, thylakoid space. Chloroplasts move along the cytoskeleton and pinch in two.

    Peroxisome · Fig. 4.19

    A sealed room for a dangerous reaction.

    A peroxisome, wrapped in one membrane, moves hydrogen from molecules to O₂, making hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), then destroys it with an enzyme that turns it into water. Liver peroxisomes detoxify alcohol this way.

    Keeping the enzymes that make H₂O₂ and those that remove it together, in one compartment, protects the rest of the cell. The dense core is thought to be packed enzyme.

    The cytoskeleton

    Support and motility · Fig. 4.20

    Not floating: anchored.

    Early electron micrographs suggested organelles drifted freely. Better microscopes revealed the cytoskeleton: a network of protein fibres that holds organelles, gives the cell its shape, like the poles of a dome tent, and moves things.

    It can be dismantled in one place and rebuilt in another, changing the cell's shape. Animal cells, with no wall, depend on it most.

    Microtubules · 25 nm

    Hollow tubes of tubulin.

    Microtubules are hollow rods of tubulin dimers, each made of an α-tubulin and a β-tubulin, in 13 columns round a 15-nm hollow. They resist compression.

    They shape the cell, separate chromosomes in division and act as tracks for organelles.

    Built and torn down

    Grow at one end, fall apart at the other.

    Microtubules grow by adding tubulin dimers and can disassemble just as quickly, so their tubulin can be reused elsewhere in the cell.

    Motor proteins · Fig. 4.21

    Feet that walk on tracks.

    A motor protein bound to a receptor on a vesicle walks along a microtubule: one foot holds while the other steps, each step paid for with ATP. This is how vesicles of neurotransmitter reach the tips of axons.

    This motor, kinesin, steps 8 nm, the length of one tubulin dimer.

    Microfilaments · 7 nm

    Two twisted strands of actin.

    Microfilaments (actin filaments) are solid, thin and bear tension. A network under the membrane holds the cell's shape; bundles stiffen microvilli; with myosin they contract muscle, crawl amoebas forward and stream the cytoplasm of plant cells.

    Intermediate filaments · 8–12 nm

    The permanent frame.

    Intermediate filaments are ropes of fibrous proteins such as keratin. More permanent than the other two, they fix organelles in place: the nucleus sits in a cage of them, and they form the nuclear lamina.

    Found only in some animals, including vertebrates. The outer layer of skin is dead cells full of keratin filaments.

    Centrosome · Fig. 4.22

    Where microtubules begin.

    In animal cells, microtubules grow from the centrosome near the nucleus. Inside it is a pair of centrioles at right angles, each nine triplets of microtubules in a ring, about 250 nm across.

    Cilia and flagella

    Moving cells, moving fluid

    Oars and tails.

    Cilia are short and many; they beat with a power stroke and a recovery stroke, like oars. Flagella are longer and few; they undulate like the tail of a fish.

    Cilia lining the trachea sweep mucus and debris out of the lungs; cilia in the oviduct move an egg toward the uterus.

    “9 + 2”

    Nine doublets round two singles.

    Under the membrane sheath: nine doublets of microtubules in a ring around a central pair. Flexible cross-linking proteins, including the radial spokes, hold them together.

    Basal body

    Anchored by a centriole's twin.

    The basal body that anchors the axoneme is built like a centriole: nine triplets, “9 + 0”. The central pair stops above it.

    In humans, the basal body of the fertilizing sperm's flagellum enters the egg and becomes a centriole.

    Answer · B · Dynein

    Walking, restrained, becomes bending.

    Dyneins have two feet: one holds while the other releases and grabs one step farther, powered by ATP. With the links cut, the doublets telescope apart. Held together, they can only bend.

    Primary cilium

    An antenna.

    Nearly every vertebrate cell has one non-moving primary cilium, “9 + 0”, whose membrane proteins pass signals inward. Cilium signalling is crucial to brain function and embryonic development.

    Walls and matrix

    Plant cell walls

    Fibres in a matrix.

    The cell wall protects the plant cell, keeps its shape and stops it taking up too much water. Cellulose synthase in the plasma membrane spins out microfibrils of cellulose, which set in a matrix of other polysaccharides and protein.

    The same design as steel-reinforced concrete or fibreglass. Walls are 0.1 µm to several µm thick, far thicker than the plasma membrane.

    Primary · middle lamella · secondary

    Laid down in order.

    A young cell secretes a thin, flexible primary wall. Between neighbours, a middle lamella of sticky pectins glues them together. Grown cells may add a secondary wall inside, in laminated layers.

    Wood is mostly secondary walls. Pectin thickens jams and jellies.

    The extracellular matrix · Fig. 4.26

    No wall: a matrix.

    Animal cells secrete an extracellular matrix (ECM). Collagen, the most abundant glycoprotein, forms strong fibres woven into a web of proteoglycan complexes: hundreds of proteoglycans on one long polysaccharide.

    Collagen is about 40% of all protein in the human body. A proteoglycan can be 95% carbohydrate.

    Integrins

    Bolted through the membrane.

    Fibronectin binds the matrix to integrins, two-part proteins that span the membrane and grip microfilaments inside. Integrins integrate: they pass signals between outside and inside.

    Through fibronectin, integrins and microfilaments, mechanical and chemical signals reach the nucleus and change which proteins the cell makes. Embryonic cells migrate by lining their microfilaments up with the matrix's grain.

    Cell junctions

    Plasmodesmata · Fig. 4.27

    Walls, with doors.

    Plasmodesmata are channels through plant cell walls, lined with plasma membrane that runs continuously from cell to cell and filled with cytosol. Water and small solutes pass freely; sometimes proteins and RNA.

    They unify most of a plant into one living continuum.

    Animal junctions · Fig. 4.28

    Three ways to join.

    In epithelia, the sheets of cells lining the body's surfaces, neighbours are joined by tight junctions, desmosomes and gap junctions. Here, the lining of the intestine.

    Tight junctions

    A seal.

    At tight junctions, specific proteins press neighbouring membranes together in continuous belts. Fluid poured on top can't leak between the cells. Skin is watertight because of them.

    Desmosomes

    Rivets.

    Desmosomes fasten cells into strong sheets, anchored inside by intermediate filaments of tough keratin. Some “muscle tears” are ruptured desmosomes.

    Channels from cell to cell.

    Gap junctions are pores of membrane proteins through which ions, sugars, amino acids and other small molecules pass from cytoplasm to cytoplasm. Heart muscle and embryos depend on them.

    Concept summary · after Campbell Biology in Focus, chapter 4

    §4.8 · A macrophage

    One cell, every system at once.

    A macrophage, a white blood cell, crawls toward a bacterium and reaches out with thin extensions, filopodia, pushed by actin filaments.

    Engulf · digest

    Swallowed, then dissolved.

    The membrane wraps the bacterium into a phagocytic vesicle. Lysosomes, made by the endomembrane system, fuse with it and digest it.

    Who made the tools?

    Every part depends on the others.

    The lysosomes' enzymes and the cytoskeleton's proteins were made on ribosomes, programmed by genetic messages sent from the DNA in the nucleus. All of it runs on ATP from mitochondria.

    Concept summary

    Every part, one page.

    Structure fits function, from the nucleus to the junctions.

    Emergent properties

    Life appears at the level of the cell.

    No organelle is alive on its own. The cell's functions arise from the order among its parts.

    A cell is greater than the sum of its parts.
